\begin{abstract}
This paper proposes a risk-aware optimization framework in which firms' sensitivity to uncertainty serves as a tunable coordination variable. We show that, for a class of stochastic market games, there exists a structured relationship between firms' risk parameters and the resulting Nash equilibrium. Based on this insight, we develop distributed algorithms that allow firms to adapt their risk parameters using only local computations and minimal information exchange. The proposed approach preserves the decentralized nature of the market: firms remain self-interested in their primary decisions, resulting in Pareto-consistent Nash equilibria, and convergence of the distributed algorithms is shown under mild conditions.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Competitive markets operating under uncertainty often exhibit a fundamental tension between individual rationality and collective efficiency.
In settings such as renewable energy markets, firms must make production or bidding decisions while facing stochastic prices and incomplete information
\cite{dvorkin2020chance, wong2007pricing, mieth2020distribution}.
When each firm optimizes its own expected profit independently, the resulting Nash equilibrium generally fails to achieve socially optimal outcomes,
even when all firms behave rationally
\cite{zhang2016competition, lee2015rational}.
This inefficiency is further exacerbated under uncertainty, where firms’ attitudes toward risk significantly influence their strategic behavior
\cite{asplund2002risk, wambach1999bertrand}.

Existing studies on stochastic Cournot and oligopoly games have examined the impact of uncertainty and risk preferences on equilibrium outcomes,
typically treating risk attitudes as fixed parameters
\cite{cheng2002bertrand, sakai1989risk, gulpinar2014analysis}.
Separately, Pareto efficiency has been studied either through centralized optimization or cooperative formulations that require full information sharing
\cite{hobbs2001individual, zhao2010social, su2018integrated}.
However, how \emph{risk sensitivity itself} can be leveraged as a coordination mechanism to reconcile Nash equilibrium behavior with Pareto-optimal outcomes—
particularly in distributed and information-limited environments—remains largely unexplored

This paper proposes a risk-aware optimization framework in which firms’ sensitivity to uncertainty serves as a tunable coordination variable.
We show that, for a class of stochastic market games, there exists a structured relationship between firms’ risk parameters and the resulting Nash equilibrium
such that appropriately chosen risk sensitivities induce Nash equilibria that lie on the Pareto frontier

In this sense, risk is not merely a preference parameter but a mechanism through which selfish agents can be guided toward efficient collective outcomes
without violating incentive compatibility.

Building on this insight, we develop distributed algorithms that allow firms to adapt their risk parameters using only local information exchange.
The proposed approach preserves the decentralized nature of the market: firms remain self-interested in their primary decisions,
while global efficiency emerges through consensus-based coordination on aggregate quantities
\cite{molzahn2017survey, yang2019survey}.
Analytical results establish existence and characterization of Pareto-consistent Nash equilibria,
and convergence of the distributed algorithms is shown under mild conditions.
Numerical examples illustrate how risk-aware adaptation shifts equilibrium behavior from inefficient Nash outcomes to Pareto-optimal payoffs,
highlighting the role of fairness and coordination under uncertainty.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
Section II formulates the stochastic market model and risk-aware objectives.
Section III characterizes the Nash equilibrium under fixed risk parameters.
Section IV establishes conditions under which Nash equilibria achieve Pareto efficiency through structured risk allocation.
Section V presents distributed algorithms based on consensus and feedback adaptation to realize these outcomes.
Numerical results are provided in Section VI, followed by concluding remarks in Section VII.

\section{Problem Formulation}

In this paper, we consider the simple case that all traditional generation assets are owned by a single utility company\footnote{The proposed results apply to the general case of multiple utility companies of individual cost functions except for notational complexity.} while renewable generation are operated by individual entities (one of which could be the utility company). And, the utility company and the renewable generation operators are the participants in both day-ahead and real-time electricity markets for profit. 

Electricity markets aim to optimize a composite objective function in the form of 
\begin{equation} 
J(P_g) = a_2 P_g^2 + a_1 P_g + a_0, \label{eq:Jm}
\end{equation}
where $a_i$ are cost coefficients, and $P_g$ is the aggregated power of traditional generation. For simplicity, marginal costs of operating renewables and battery storage sites are modeled as zero, and hence they need not to be included explicitly in objective function \eqref{eq:Jm}. 

Let $\Omega$ be the set of renewable generation operators, with $n=|\Omega|$ being its cardinal. For the day-ahead market, their renewable generations, $P_j$ for $j\in\Omega$, are modeled as stochastic variables, and similarly the grid total load $P_L$ is also modeled as a stochastic variable. Lets form the whole vector of random variables as 
\begin{equation} 
x = \begin{bmatrix} P_1 & \cdots & P_n & P_L \end{bmatrix}^T.  \label{eq:x}
\end{equation}
We assume that probability distribution of random vector $x$ is Gaussian as follows:
\[ f(x) = (2\pi)^{-\frac{n+1}{2}}\Sigma^{-\frac{1}{2}} e^{-\frac{1}{2}(x-x^o)^T\Sigma^{-1}(x-x^o)},  \]
where  $x^o=\mathbb{E}[x]$ is the vector of forecast (for the day-ahead market), and 
\begin{eqnarray*}
\Sigma & = & \mathbb{E}[(x-x^o)(x-x^o)^T] \\
& = & \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_{11} & \cdots & \sigma_{1n} & \sigma_{1L} \\ \vdots & \cdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ \sigma_{n1} & \cdots & \sigma_{nn} & \sigma_{nL} \\ \sigma_{1L} & \cdots & \sigma_{nL} & \sigma_{LL}
\end{bmatrix},  
\end{eqnarray*}
is the covariance matrix with $\sigma_{ij}=\sigma_{ji}$. In the covariance matrix, the last row and column correspond to covariances between the renewable generations and the load while $\sigma_{LL}=\sigma_L^2>0$ is the variance of the load. It is known that 
$\sigma_{ii}=\sigma_i^2>0$, $\sigma_{ij}=\rho_{ij}\sigma_i\sigma_j$, $\sigma_{iL}=\rho_{iL}\sigma_i\sigma_L$, where $\rho_{ij},\rho_{iL}\in[0,1]$. Typically, $\rho_{ij},\rho_{iL}\not=0$ because renewable generations and load are correlated through the projected weather condition. 

The day ahead market clears the projected aggregated power generation $P_g^o$ and its associated price $\lambda^o$ by minimizing $J(P_g^o)$ under the following power balance constraint:
\begin{equation} 
P_g^o + \sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_j x_j^o  = x_{n+1}^o, \label{eq:DA_power}
\end{equation}
where $x_{n+1}^o=P_L^o$, $\alpha_j\geq0$, and $\alpha_j x_j^o =\alpha_j P_j^o$ represent the bids by the renewable generation operators into the day-ahead market. Similarly, the real-time market clears the aggregated power generation $P_g$ and its associated price $\lambda$ by minimizing $J(P_g)$ under the following real-time power balance constraint:
\begin{equation} 
P_g + \sum_{j=1}^n x_j   = x_{n+1}. \label{eq:RT_power}
\end{equation}
It is well known that, with global information, day-ahead price $\lambda^o$ and real-time $\lambda$ are the dual variables associated with constraints \eqref{eq:DA_power} and \eqref{eq:RT_power}, respectively. 

The renewable generation operators aim to maximize the following objectives\footnote{Strictly speaking, we should use $J_i^{\prime} = \Pi_i - \beta_i \mathbb{E}[(\Pi_i - \mathbb{E}[\Pi_i])^2]$ which also yields a second-order polynomial in $\alpha_i$ as does \eqref{eq:J_i} but involves calculations of higher moments. Accordingly, \eqref{eq:J_i} is adopted instead.} individually:
\begin{subequations}
\begin{equation} 
J_i = \Pi_i - \beta_i \mathbb{E}[(\lambda - \lambda^o)^2], \label{eq:J_i}
\end{equation}
where $\beta_i\geq0$, 
\begin{equation} 
\Pi_i %= \lambda^o \alpha_i P_i^o + \mathbb{E}[\lambda (P_i - \alpha_i P_i^o)] 
= \lambda^o \alpha_i x_i^o + \mathbb{E}[\lambda (x_i - \alpha_i x_i^o)], \label{eq:Pi_i}
\end{equation}
\end{subequations}
and the pair $\{\alpha_i, \; \beta_i\}$ represents the bidding strategy of the $i$-th operator into the electricity markets. If $\beta_i=0$ is chosen, the $i$-th entity aims to maximize its average profit from the total revenues from participating in the day ahead and real time markets. By selecting $\beta_i>0$, the $i$-th entity also attempts to reduce its profit variation by suppressing variations of real time price $\lambda$ around day ahead price $\lambda^o$.  By choosing $\beta_i<0$, the $i$th entity tries to maximize its profit by encouraging more variations of real time price $\lambda$ around day ahead price $\lambda^o$. In other words, the $i$-th entity is risk averse if $\beta_i>0$ and risk seeking if $\beta_i<0$.

The objective of this paper is to show the following advantages by introducing the risk aware bidding strategy of $\{\alpha_i, \; \beta_i\}$: \textcolor{red}{ADD}. Power grid congestion is not considered in the paper but can be considered. 

\section{Centralized Risk-Aware Solution Under Global Information}

The electricity markets are bi-level optimization problems linked together by dual variables. At the upper level, they are quadratic optimization problems with equality constraints, that is, the markets perform their economic dispatch with the following Lagrangians: 
\begin{subequations}
\begin{equation} 
L^o = a_2(P_g^o)^2+a_1P_g^o+a_0 + \lambda^o\left(x_{n+1}^o- P_g^o-\sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_j x_j^o \right), \label{eq:L*}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation} 
L = a_2P_g^2+a_1P_g+a_0 + \lambda^o\left(x_{n+1}-P_g-\sum_{j=1}^n x_j\right). \label{eq:L}
\end{equation}
\end{subequations}
Applying the first-order optimality condition to the Lagrangians yields the following solutions for the prices:
\begin{subequations}
\begin{equation} 
\lambda^o = 2a_2P_g^o +a_1 = 2a_2\left(x_{n+1}^o-\sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_j x_j^o \right) +a_1 , \label{eq:lambda*}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation} 
\lambda = 2a_2P_g+a_1=2a_2\left(x_{n+1}-\sum_{j=1}^n x_j \right) + a_1. \label{eq:lambda}
\end{equation}
\end{subequations}

The lower level optimization problems are also quadratic, and the centralized solutions under global information are summarized into the following lemma and theorem. Lemma 1 provides the solution of cooperative optimization when all the entities bind themselves together and act as one. In this case, the best outcome for the group is achieved under the risk neutral strategy.

\noindent {\bf Lemma 1:} {\it Consider the case that all renewable generation entities form a coalition by sharing all the information and setting $\alpha_i=\alpha$ and $\beta_i=\frac{1}{n}\beta$, respectively. Then, for any choice of $\beta\in[-(4a_2)^{-1},\; +\infty)$, the optimal bidding strategy to optimize objective $J_i$ in \eqref{eq:J_i} is
\begin{equation}
\alpha^* = \frac{1 + 4a_2 \beta}{2(1 + 2a_2\beta)}.
\label{eq:alpha_1}
\end{equation}
In addition, by further choosing $\beta_i$ to optimize $\Pi_i$ in \eqref{eq:Pi_i}, the optimal bidding strategy $\{\alpha^*, \; \beta^*\}$ is given by}
\begin{equation}
\alpha^*  =  \frac{1}{2}, \quad
\beta^*  =  0.
\label{eq:optimal_1}
\end{equation}

\noindent {\bf Proof:} It follows from \eqref{eq:Pi_i} and \eqref{eq:lambda*} that, if $\alpha_i=\alpha$, 
\begin{eqnarray*}
\Pi_r & = & \sum_{j=1}^n \Pi_j = \lambda^o \alpha x_r^o +  \mathbb{E}[\lambda (x_r-\alpha x_r^o)] \\
 \lambda^o   & = & 2a_2\left(x_{n+1}^o-\alpha x_r^o \right) +a_1,
\end{eqnarray*}
where $x_r = \sum_{j=1}^n x_j$ is the total renewable generation, and $x_r^o = \sum_{j=1}^n x_j^o$. Substituting \eqref{eq:lambda} into the above equations yeld
\begin{eqnarray}
\Pi_r & = &  [2a_2\left(x_{n+1}^o-\alpha x_r^o \right) +a_1] \alpha x_r^o \nonumber \\
&& + \mathbb{E}[(2a_2\left(x_{n+1}-x_r \right) + a_1)(x_r-\alpha x_r^o)] \nonumber \\
  & = & 2a_2\alpha(1-\alpha)(x_r^o)^2 +a_1x_r^o  + 2a_2\mathbb{E}[\left(x_{n+1}x_r-x_r^2 \right)] \nonumber\\
   & = & 2a_2\alpha(1-\alpha)(x_r^o)^2 +a_1x_r^o \nonumber \\
   && + 2a_2\left(x_{n+1}^ox_r^o+\sigma_{rL}-(x_r^o)^2-\sigma_r^2 \right), \label{eq:Pi_r_exp}
\end{eqnarray}
where $\one$ is the $n$-dimensional column vector of $1$s and 
\begin{eqnarray*}
    \sigma_r^2 & = & \mathbb{E}[(x_1+\cdots+x_n-(x_1^o+\cdots+x_n^o))^2] \\
    & = & \mathbb{E}[ \one^T (x-x^o)(x-x^o)^T\one] \\
    & = & \one^T \Sigma \one. 
\end{eqnarray*}
On the other hand, it follows from \eqref{eq:J_i}, \eqref{eq:lambda*}, and \eqref{eq:lambda} that, if $\beta_i = \frac{1}{n}\beta$,
\begin{eqnarray*}
J_r & = & \sum_{j\in\Omega} J_j \\
 & = & \Pi_r  - \beta \mathbb{E}[2a_2 (x_{n+1}-x_{n+1}^o -x_r +\alpha x_r^o)^2] \\
 & = & 2a_2\alpha(1-\alpha)(x_r^o)^2  - 4a_2^2  \beta  (1-\alpha)^2 (x_r^o)^2 \\
 & & +a_1x_r^o + 2a_2\left(x_{n+1}^ox_r^o+\sigma_{rL}-(x_r^o)^2-\sigma_r^2 \right)\\
 && - 4a_2^2  \beta (\sigma_L^2+\sigma_r^2 - 2\sigma_{rL}). 
\end{eqnarray*}
Using the above expression, taking the partial derivative of $J_r$ with respect to $\alpha$, and setting the partial derivative to zero yield optimal bidding strategy \eqref{eq:alpha_1}. 

Substituting solution \eqref{eq:alpha_1} into 
\eqref{eq:Pi_r_exp} and taking the partial of $\Pi_r$ with respect to $\beta$ result in
\[ \frac{\partial \Pi_r}{\partial \beta} = - 2a_2(x_r^o)^2\frac{4a_2^2\beta}{(1+2a_2\beta)^3}, \]
which yields the optimal bidding strategy \eqref{eq:optimal_1}. This completes the proof. \hfill $\Box$

It follows from \eqref{eq:alpha_1} that
\[ \lim_{\beta\rightarrow-1/(4a_2)} \alpha = 0, \quad  \lim_{\beta\rightarrow0} \alpha = 0.5, \quad \lim_{\beta\rightarrow +\infty} \alpha = 1. \]
Since $\alpha\in[0,1]$, the design parameter $\beta$ translates an operator's risk tolerance level into a specific choice of $\alpha$ value. Although the values correspond to risk neutral choices may change, this translation is applicable to not only cooperative coalition game but also noncooperative games. 

The following theorem provides the solution to non-cooperative optimization in which each agent devises its strategy according to its own objective function $J_i$. 

\noindent {\bf Theorem 1:} {\it For renewable energy generation operators, their oligopoly game always has a Nash equilibrium. Specifically,
for any choice of $\beta_i\in[-(4a_2)^{-1},+\infty)$, the optimal bidding strategy $\alpha_i^*$ to optimize objective $J_i$ in \eqref{eq:J_i} forms a Nash equilibrium corresponding to the solution to the linear algebraic equation:
\begin{equation}
A \begin{bmatrix} \alpha_1^* & \cdots & \alpha_n^* \end{bmatrix}^T = B,
\label{eq:alpha_i}
\end{equation}
where $\gamma_{ij}=x_j^o/x_i^o$,
\[ A=[A_{ij}], \quad A_{ij} = \left\{ \begin{array}{ll} 2(1+2a_2\beta_i) & \mbox{if $i=j$} \\
(1+4a_2\beta_i)\gamma_{ij} & \mbox{if $i\not=j$}\end{array} \right., \]
and
\[ B=[B_i], \quad B_i = (1+4a_2\beta_i)\sum_{j=1}^n \gamma_{ij}. \]
In addition,  the total profit $\sum_{i=1}^n\Pi_i$ can be maximized by choosing $\beta_j$ to maximize the following function: 
\begin{equation} 
g(\alpha_1^*,\cdots,\alpha^*_n) = f(\alpha_1^*,\cdots,\alpha^*_n) \left[ 1 - f(\alpha_1^*,\cdots,\alpha^*_n)  \right](x_r^o)^2.  
\label{eq:G}
\end{equation} 
where $x_r^o=\sum_{j=1}^n x_j^o$, $\eta_i = x_i^o/x_r^o$, and 
\begin{equation} 
f(\alpha_1,\cdots,\alpha_n) \stackrel{\triangle}{=} \sum_{i=1}^n  \alpha_i^* \eta_i.  
\label{eq:F}
\end{equation} 
}

\noindent {\bf Proof:} It follows from \eqref{eq:Pi_i},  \eqref{eq:lambda*}, and \eqref{eq:lambda} that 
\begin{eqnarray}
\Pi_i & = & \left[ 2a_2\left(x_{n+1}^o-\sum_{j=1}^n \alpha_j x_j^o 
  \right) +a_1 \right] \alpha_i x_i^o \nonumber \\ && 
  + \mathbb{E}\left[\left(2a_2\left(x_{n+1}-\sum_{j=1}^n x_j \right) + a_1\right) (x_i - \alpha_i x_i^o)\right] \nonumber \\
  & = & 2a_2\sum_{j=1}^n\alpha_i(1-\alpha_j)x_j^o x_i^o +a_1 x_i^o  \nonumber \\ 
  && + 2a_2\mathbb{E}\left[\left(x_{n+1}-\sum_{j=1}^n x_j \right) x_i\right] \nonumber\\
   & = & 2a_2\sum_{j=1}^n\alpha_i(1-\alpha_j)x_j^o x_i^o +a_1 x_i^o  \nonumber \\ 
  && + 2a_2\left(x_{n+1}^o x_i^o + \sigma_{iL}- \sum_{j=1}^n (x_j^o  x_i^o+ \sigma_{ij})\right), \label{eq:Pi_j_exp}
\end{eqnarray}
where the identity $\mathbb{E}[x_ix_j]=x_i^ox_j^o+\sigma_{ij}$ is used. On the other hand, It follows from \eqref{eq:lambda*} and \eqref{eq:lambda} that 
\begin{eqnarray} 
&& \mathbb{E}[(\lambda - \lambda^o)^2] \nonumber \\
& = & 
4a_2^2  \mathbb{E}\left[\left(x_{n+1}-x_{n+1}^0 +\sum_{j=1}^n x_j-\alpha_j x_j^o\right)^2\right] \nonumber \\
& = & 4a_2^2\left[\sigma_{LL} + 2\sum_{j=1}^n \sigma_{jL} + 2 \sum_{j=1}^n \sum_{l=1}^n \sigma_{jl} \right. \nonumber \\
&& \left.+ \left(\sum_{j=1}^n (1-\alpha_j) x_j^o\right)^2 \right]
, \label{eq:sigma_lambda}
\end{eqnarray}
The expression of $J_i$ defined in \eqref{eq:J_i} becomes explicit in terms of $\alpha_i$ by combining \eqref{eq:Pi_j_exp} and \eqref{eq:sigma_lambda}. Taking the partial derivatives of $J_i$ with respect to $\alpha_i$ yields
\begin{subequations}
\begin{eqnarray} 
\frac{\partial J_i}{\partial \alpha_i} & = &  2a_2(1+4a_2\beta_i) \sum_{j=1}^n(1-\alpha_j)x_j^o x_i^o \nonumber \\
&& - 2a_2 \alpha_i (x_i^o)^2, \label{eq:p1_Ji} \\
\frac{\partial J^2_i}{\partial \alpha_i^2} & = & - (4a_2+8a_2^2\beta_i) (x_i^o)^2 <0 \;\;\; \forall \beta_i\geq0. \label{eq:p2_Ji}
\end{eqnarray}
\end{subequations}
Inequality \eqref{eq:p2_Ji} shows that Nash equilibrium exists, and setting \eqref{eq:p1_Ji} to be zero yields equation \eqref{eq:alpha_i} to solve for Nash equilibrium. 

It follows from \eqref{eq:alpha_i} that $\alpha_i^*$ are functions of $\beta_j$. We know from  \eqref{eq:Pi_j_exp} that 
\begin{eqnarray}
    \Pi & = & \sum_i \Pi_i \nonumber \\
     & = & 2a_2\left[ g(\alpha_1,\cdots,\alpha_n) - \left(x_r^o\right)^2 + x_{n+1}^o x_r^o\right]  +a_1 x_r^o  \nonumber \\ 
     && + 2a_2 \left[ \sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_{iL} -\sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n\sigma_{ij}\right], \label{eq:Pi_exp}
\end{eqnarray}
where
\[ g(\alpha_1,\cdots,\alpha_n) \stackrel{\triangle}{=} \sum_{i=1}^n  \alpha_i x_i^o  \sum_{j=1}^n (1-\alpha_j ) x_j^o. \]
Hence, $\Pi$ is maximized if function $g(\cdot)$ is maximized by choosing $\beta_j$.
Simplifying the above expression of $g(\cdot)$ yields \eqref{eq:G}. This completes the proof. \hfill $\Box$

Theorem 1 extends the results in \cite{Chow2017} in several ways. First, load uncertainties and correlations among the renewable generations are considered.  As a result, we know from \eqref{eq:Pi_exp} that the degradation of total profit function $\Pi$ due to uncertainties is 
\[ \Delta \Pi = 2a_2 \left[ \sum_{i=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n\sigma_{ij}- \sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_{iL}\right], \]
is less as long as there is some correlation (e.g., due to weather) between renewable generation and load variation. Second, risk aware bidding strategies are considered, specifically, theorem 1 allows us to study and compare three classes of bidding strategies: risk neutral, risk adverse, and risk seeking. The latter two correspond to optimization of design parameters $\beta_j$. 

Theorem 1 shows that the Nash equilibrium in terms of design parameters $\beta_j$ can be further optimized, and the optimal choices of $\beta_j$ are the main subject of this paper. To illustrate some of the specifics, lets first consider the two simple cases: $n=1$, and $n=2$. The corresponding results are provided by the subsequent corollaries, followed by explanations. 

\noindent  {\bf Corollary 1:} {\it If $n=1$, the optimal biding strategy becomes the same as that in lemma 1 and with $g(\alpha^*)=\frac{1}{4} (x_r^o)^2$.}

\noindent  {\bf Corollary 2:} {\it In the case of duopoly ($n=2$), the Nash bidding strategies with respect to $J_i$ are:
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:2p-alpha}
\begin{equation}
\alpha_1^*= \frac{(1+4a_2\beta_1)}{3+4a_2(\beta_1+\beta_2)}(1+\gamma_{12}), 
\label{eq:2p-alpha1}
\end{equation} 
\begin{equation}
 \alpha_2^*= \frac{(1+4a_2\beta_2)}{3+4a_2(\beta_1+\beta_2)}(1+\gamma_{21}), \, 
\label{eq:2p-alpha2}
\end{equation} 
\end{subequations}
Furthermore, $\beta_i$ can be optimized with respect to $(\Pi_1+\Pi_2)$, and the outcomes are strategically dependent as follows. 
\begin{itemize}
\item Risk neutral (RN) optimal bidding strategy ($\beta_i^{NR}=0$ for $i=1,2$) and the corresponding optimized profit is:
\begin{equation}
g^{RN}(\alpha_1^*, \alpha_2^*)  =  \frac{2}{9}(x_r^o)^2. 
\label{eq:2p-neutral}
\end{equation} 
\item Risk seeking (RS) optimal bidding strategy  and the corresponding enhanced optimal profit are:
\begin{subequations}
\label{eq:2p-RS}
\begin{equation}
\beta_1^{RS}+\beta_2^{RS}=-\frac{1}{4a_2},
\label{eq:2p-RS-beta}
\end{equation} 
\begin{equation}
 g^{RS}(\alpha_1^*,\alpha_2^*)= \frac{1}{4}(x_r^o)^2 > g^{RN}(\alpha_1^*, \alpha_2^*). 
\label{eq:2p-RS-g}
\end{equation} 
\end{subequations}
\end{itemize} }

 \noindent {\bf Proof:} It follows from \eqref{eq:alpha_i} that 
 \begin{eqnarray*}
  && \begin{bmatrix} 2(1+2a_2 \beta_1) & (1+4a_2\beta_1)\gamma_{12}  \\ (1+4a_2\beta_2)\gamma_{21}  & 2(1+2a_2 \beta_2)  \end{bmatrix}   
  \begin{bmatrix}  \alpha_1^* \\ \alpha_2^* \end{bmatrix}  \\
  & = &  \begin{bmatrix}  (1+4a_2\beta_1)(1+\gamma_{12}) \\ (1+4a_2\beta_2)(1+\gamma_{21}) \end{bmatrix},
      \end{eqnarray*}
 whose solution is given by \eqref{eq:2p-alpha}.

It follows from \eqref{eq:2p-alpha} that, when $\beta_i=0$,
\[ \alpha_1^* = \frac{1}{3}(1+\gamma_{12}), \quad \alpha_2^* = \frac{1}{3}(1+\gamma_{21}). \]
Substituting the above solutions into \eqref{eq:F} and \eqref{eq:G} yields \eqref{eq:2p-neutral}.

To further optimize the risk-aware bidding strategy \eqref{eq:2p-alpha}, we rewrite the solution as
\[ \alpha_1^* = \xi_1 (1+\gamma_{12}), \quad \alpha_2^* = \xi_2(1+\gamma_{21}), \]
where 
\begin{equation}
    \xi_i = \frac{(1+4a_2\beta_i)}{3+4a_2(\beta_1+\beta_2)}. \label{eq:xi}
\end{equation} 
It follows from \eqref{eq:F} and \eqref{eq:G} that 
\begin{eqnarray*}
    g(\alpha_1^*, \alpha_2^*) & = & (\xi_1+\xi_2) [1- (\xi_1+\xi_2)] (x_r^o)^2, 
\end{eqnarray*}
which is maximized to be that in \eqref{eq:2p-RS-g} if
\[ \xi_1+\xi_2=\frac{1}{2}. \]
Invoking \eqref{eq:xi} and solving for the optimal values of $\beta_i$ yields $\beta_i^{RS}$ in \eqref{eq:2p-RS-beta}. This completes the proof. \hfill $\Box$

The significant outcome of theorem 1 and its corollaries is that, while the profit under noncooperative risk neutral Nash equilibrium is typically worse than that of cooperative coalition solution, risk seeking Nash strategies with $(\beta_1+\beta_2)$ being an optimal value can be employed to recover the same profit as the cooperative coalition's. This includes the special but fair strategy that all the entities employ the same risk seeking level. In what follows, this strategy is to be developed via distributed optimization with {\it minimum} information exchange/observation.   

\section{Risk Aware Nash Solutions with Pareto Payoff}

The results in Section~III establish that, for any fixed choice of the
risk parameters $\{\beta_i\}_{i=1}^n$, the noncooperative bidding game
among renewable generators admits a unique Nash equilibrium in terms of
the bidding coefficients $\{\alpha_i^\ast\}_{i=1}^n$.
However, the resulting Nash equilibrium is generally not Pareto optimal
with respect to the total expected profit.

In this section, we show that the Nash equilibrium can be systematically
shifted to a Pareto-optimal operating point by appropriately selecting
the risk parameters $\{\beta_i\}$.
Importantly, this Pareto enhancement does not require coordination on
the bidding variables $\alpha_i$ themselves, but only on the aggregate
risk exposure across agents.

\noindent\textbf{Theorem 2:} There exists a family of risk parameter allocations $(\beta_1^*,\ldots,\beta_n^*)$ such that the corresponding Nash equilibrium $(\alpha_1^*,\ldots,\alpha_n^*)$ satisfying \eqref{eq:alpha_i} achieves the maximum aggregate profit:
\[
g(\alpha_1^*,\ldots,\alpha_n^*) = g^{\max} = \frac{1}{4}(x_r^o)^2.
\]
Specifically, any risk parameters satisfying
\begin{equation}
f(\alpha_1^*(\boldsymbol{\beta}),\ldots,\alpha_n^*(\boldsymbol{\beta})) = \frac{1}{2}, \label{eq:pareto_condition}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_i^*(\boldsymbol{\beta})$ denote the Nash equilibrium strategies corresponding to $\boldsymbol{\beta} = (\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_n)$, will induce a Pareto-optimal Nash equilibrium.

\noindent\textbf{Proof:} Define the mapping $\Phi: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ by $\Phi(\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_n) = f(\alpha_1^*(\boldsymbol{\beta}),\ldots,\alpha_n^*(\boldsymbol{\beta}))$, where $\alpha_i^*(\boldsymbol{\beta})$ are determined by the Nash equilibrium conditions \eqref{eq:alpha_i}. Since the matrix $A$ in \eqref{eq:alpha_i} is invertible for $\beta_i > -\frac{1}{4a_2}$ (as shown in the proof of Theorem~1), the Nash equilibrium depends continuously on $(\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_n)$, and hence $\Phi$ is continuous.

We establish existence by showing that $\Phi$ can take values both below and above $\frac{1}{2}$:
\begin{itemize}
\item For the risk-neutral case $\beta_i = 0$ for all $i$ with symmetric players, $\Phi(0,\ldots,0) = \frac{n}{n+1} > \frac{1}{2}$ for $n \geq 1$.
\item As $\beta_i \to -\frac{1}{4a_2}$ uniformly for all $i$, the Nash equilibrium yields $\alpha_i^* \to 0$, giving $\Phi(\boldsymbol{\beta}) \to 0 < \frac{1}{2}$.
\end{itemize}

By the intermediate value theorem, there exists $(\beta_1^*,\ldots,\beta_n^*)$ such that $\Phi(\beta_1^*,\ldots,\beta_n^*) = \frac{1}{2}$, achieving $g = g^{\max}$. \hfill$\Box$


\noindent {\bf Theorem 3:}
\label{thm:pareto_nash}
The risk-aware Nash equilibrium can be enhanced to yield a Pareto-optimal total profit if and only if the risk parameters satisfy
\begin{equation}
\sum_{i=1}^n \beta_i = \frac{1-n}{4a_2},
\label{eq:beta_sum_condition}
\end{equation}

\noindent {\bf Proof:}
At the Nash equilibrium, the bidding coefficients satisfied
\[
\alpha_i^\ast \eta_i
= (1+4a_2\beta_i)\bigl(1-f(\alpha^\ast)\bigr)
\]

Summing over all $i$ yields
\[
f(\alpha^\ast)
= \bigl(1-f(\alpha^\ast)\bigr)
\left(n + 4a_2\sum_{i=1}^n \beta_i\right).
\]

Solving for $f(\alpha^\ast)$ gives
\[
f(\alpha^\ast)
= \frac{n + 4a_2\sum_{i=1}^n \beta_i}{1 + n + 4a_2\sum_{i=1}^n \beta_i}.
\]

The Pareto-optimality condition \eqref{eq:pareto_condition} holds if and
only if
\[
n + 4a_2\sum_{i=1}^n \beta_i = 1,
\]

The Pareto condition constrains only the aggregate risk exposure; therefore, infinitely many heterogeneous risk allocations $\{\beta_i\}$ yield Pareto-optimal Nash equilibria. 

\noindent {\bf Corollary 4 ($n=1$):}
If there is only one renewable generator, the Pareto-optimal Nash condition reduces to
\[
\beta_1 = 0,
\]
corresponding to a risk-neutral bidding strategy.

This result confirms that, in the absence of competition, risk-seeking
behavior provides no strategic advantage. The Pareto-optimal Nash
equilibrium coincides with the profit-maximizing solution of the single agent, consistent with Lemma~1 and standard monopoly pricing intuition.

\noindent {\bf Corollary 5 ($n=2$):}
For two competing renewable generators, the Pareto-optimal Nash equilibrium satisfies
\[
\beta_1 + \beta_2 = -\frac{1}{4a_2}.
\]

This condition admits infinitely many Pareto-optimal risk allocations.
In particular, the symmetric and fair solution
$\beta_1 = \beta_2 = -\tfrac{1}{8a_2}$
yields a risk-seeking Nash equilibrium that achieves the same total
profit as the centralized cooperative solution, despite the absence of
explicit coordination.

\noindent {\bf Corollary 6 ($n=\infty$):}
As the number of renewable generators grows large, the Pareto-optimal condition implies that the aggregate risk parameter
$\sum_{i=1}^n \beta_i$ diverges to negative infinity. To interpret this
result on a per-agent basis, consider the average risk sensitivity
\[
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \beta_i
= \frac{1-n}{4a_2 n}
= -\frac{1}{4a_2} + \frac{1}{4a_2 n}.
\]
Taking the limit as $n \to \infty$, we obtain
\[
\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \beta_i
= -\frac{1}{4a_2},
\]
which coincides with the lower bound on admissible risk parameters
established in Lemma~1.

Since admissible risk parameters must satisfy
$\beta_i \ge -\frac{1}{4a_2}$ to ensure existence of the Nash equilibrium,
this result shows that, as the number of renewable generators grows,
Pareto-optimality forces the \emph{average} risk sensitivity to approach
the feasibility boundary from above. In the large-population limit,
socially optimal performance is therefore achieved when the collective
risk exposure saturates this lower bound, even though individual agents
may remain strictly feasible for any finite $n$.

This reveals an emergent mean-field structure: rather than requiring
coordination of individual bidding strategies, Pareto efficiency is
recovered through convergence of the aggregate risk profile to its
feasibility limit.

\noindent {\bf Remark 1 (Capacity Constraints and Overbidding):}
The Nash equilibrium formula~\eqref{eq:lemma2} implicitly assumes that the resulting bidding coefficients satisfy $\alpha_i^* \in [0,1]$. However, for heterogeneous risk allocations, particularly when some operators are risk-seeking (with $\beta_i'$ near the lower bound $-1/4$) while others are risk-averse (with $\beta_i' > 0$), the formula may yield $\alpha_i^* > 1$ for risk-averse operators.

To ensure $\alpha_i^* \leq 1$ at the Pareto-optimal sum $\sum_j \beta_j' = (1-n)/4$, substituting into~\eqref{eq:lemma2} yields the constraint:
\[
\beta_i' \leq \frac{2\eta_i - 1}{4}, \quad \forall i.
\]
Since $\eta_i = x_i^o / x_r^o < 1$ for typical capacity distributions, this upper bound is negative for all operators. Consequently, Pareto-optimal Nash equilibria without capacity saturation require all operators to be mildly risk-seeking.

When risk-averse operators violate this bound, they would theoretically ``overbid'' beyond their capacity. In practice, such operators are constrained to $\alpha_i = 1$, which modifies the equilibrium structure. Interestingly, simulations show that the distributed algorithm can still achieve Pareto-optimal aggregate profit $\sum \Pi_i$ by driving $f(\alpha) \to 1/2$ through clamped bidding, though the theoretical relationship $\sum \beta_j' = (1-n)/4$ no longer holds exactly. This saturating equilibrium represents an alternative pathway to Pareto efficiency when heterogeneous risk preferences span a wide range.

\section{Distributed Risk Aware Solutions}

The previous analysis shows that the total profit $\Pi$ corresponding to the Nash equilibrium \eqref{eq:alpha_i} can be improved by optimizing function \eqref{eq:G}. The optimization process is constructed in such a way that {\it minimum} information is shared. To this end, let us define
\[ \beta_i^{\prime} = a_2 \beta_i, \;\;\; i=1,\cdots,n. \] 
This means that objective functions in \eqref{eq:J_i} is expressed as 
\begin{equation} 
J_i = \Pi_i - \frac{\beta_i^{\prime} }{a_2} \mathbb{E}[(\lambda - \lambda^o)^2], \label{eq:betap_i}
\end{equation}
in which the physical meaning of $\beta_i^{\prime}$ remains identically the same as that of $\beta_i$.
This substitution removes the need of sharing private cost information about the traditional generation assets. The following lemma and theorem provide specific algorithms for distributively solving for the risk-aware Nash equilibrium (under fixed choices of $\beta_i$) and the optimized risk-aware Nash solution (via optimization of $\beta_i$), respectively.   

\noindent {\bf Lemma 2:}  {\it Under fixed choices of $\beta_i$, the risk-aware Nash equilibrium in \eqref{eq:alpha_i} can be solved as follows:}

\begin{equation}
\alpha_i^*
= \frac{1+4\beta_i'}{\eta_i\bigl(1 + n + 4\sum_{j=1}^n \beta_j'\bigr)}
\label{eq:lemma2}
\end{equation}

\noindent {\bf Proof:} It follows from \eqref{eq:p1_Ji} that the Nash equilibrium is to be solved according to the equation 
\[ (1+4\beta_i^{\prime}) \sum_{j=1}^n(1-\alpha_j)x_j^o - \alpha_i x_i^o =0, \]
which can be rewritten as 
\begin{equation} 
  (1+4\beta_i^{\prime}) [ 1- f(\alpha_1,\cdots,\alpha_n)] - \alpha_i \eta_i =0,
\label{eq:NE_equation}
\end{equation} 
where function $f(\cdot)$ is defined in \eqref{eq:F}, and $\eta_i=x_i^o/x_r^o$. 

Lemma~2 implies that, for fixed $\beta_i'$, the Nash equilibrium in $\alpha_i$ can be computed distributively through a scalar-coupled iteration involving only local parameters $\beta_i'$ and the aggregate signal $f(\alpha)$.

\subsection{Consensus Protocol for Aggregate Quantities}

To compute the Pareto-optimal strategies, operators need to determine key aggregate quantities through consensus. We employ a standard average consensus protocol where each operator $i$ maintains a state variable $z_i(k)$ at iteration $k$, initialized with its local value. The consensus update is:
\begin{equation}
z_i(k+1) = z_i(k) + \epsilon \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}_i} [z_j(k) - z_i(k)], \label{eq:consensus}
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon > 0$ is the consensus gain satisfying $\epsilon < \frac{1}{\max_i |\mathcal{N}_i|}$ for convergence, and $\mathcal{N}_i$ denotes the set of neighbors of operator $i$ in the communication graph.

\noindent {\bf Lemma 3:}

Under a connected communication graph, the consensus protocol~\eqref{eq:consensus} converges to the average:
\begin{equation}
\lim_{k \to \infty} z_i(k) = \bar{z} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n z_j(0), \quad \forall i.
\end{equation}

\noindent {\bf Proof:}
Standard result from consensus theory. The update~\eqref{eq:consensus} can be written in matrix form as $z(k+1) = Pz(k)$ where $P = I - \epsilon L$ and $L$ is the Laplacian matrix of the communication graph. Under a connected graph, $P$ is a doubly stochastic matrix with a single eigenvalue at 1, ensuring convergence to the average.

Using Lemma~3, we define the following consensus-based computations using explicit consensus variables:

\textbf{Computing $n$ using $z_n$:} Operator 1 initializes $z_{n,1}(0) = 1$ and all others initialize $z_{n,j}(0) = 0$. After consensus, $\bar{z}_n = \frac{1}{n}$, so:
\begin{equation}
n = \frac{1}{\bar{z}_n}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Computing $x_r^o$ using $z_{xr0}$:} Each operator initializes $z_{xr0,i}(0) = x_i^o$ and runs consensus to obtain $\bar{z}_{xr0} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n x_j^o$. Then:
\begin{equation}
x_r^o = n \cdot \bar{z}_{xr0}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Computing $\eta_i$ using $z_\eta$:} After obtaining $x_r^o$, each operator computes the consensus variable:
\begin{equation}
z_{\eta,i} = \frac{x_i^o}{x_r^o}, \quad \eta_i = z_{\eta,i}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{Computing $\sum_{j=1}^n \beta_j'$ using $z_\beta(k)$:} At each iteration $k$, each operator initializes $z_{\beta,i}(k) = \beta_i'(k)$ and runs consensus to obtain $\bar{z}_\beta(k) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^n \beta_j'(k)$. Then:
\begin{equation}
\sum_{j=1}^n \beta_j'(k) = n \cdot \bar{z}_\beta(k). \label{eq:beta_sum_consensus}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Algorithm Optimization}
The distributed computation of the aggregate forecast $x_r^o$ and the corresponding weights $\eta_i$ enables a fully decentralized implementation of the Nash equilibrium characterized in Lemma 2. In this subsection, we leverage this structure to design an iterative distributed algorithm that jointly computes the Nash equilibrium bidding coefficients and optimizes the risk parameters to achieve Pareto-optimal performance.

Specifically, for fixed values of $\{\beta_i'\}$, Lemma~2 provides a closed-form expression for the Nash equilibrium bidding coefficients $\{\alpha_i^*\}$ in terms of the locally available quantities $\beta_i'$ and $\eta_i$. Building on this result, the proposed algorithm alternates between updating the bidding coefficients $\alpha_i$ to track the Nash equilibrium and adjusting the risk parameters $\beta_i'$ to steer the system toward the Pareto-optimal operating point identified in Section~IV.

Assuming that the consensus process described in the previous subsection is executed sufficiently fast relative to the outer updates, each agent has access to an accurate estimate of $\eta_i$ at every iteration. Under this assumption, the following distributed nonlinear iterative scheme is implemented. For all $k \in \mathbb{N}^+$, each renewable generator updates its bidding coefficient and risk parameter using consensus variables $z_\eta$ and $z_\beta(k)$:

\begin{equation}
\alpha_i^*(k) = \frac{1 + 4\beta_i'(k)}{z_{\eta,i}} \left[\frac{1}{1 + n + 4\sum_{j=1}^n z_{\beta,j}(k)}\right], \label{eq:alpha_direct}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
f(\alpha^*(k)) = \frac{n + 4\sum_{j=1}^n z_{\beta,j}(k)}{1 + n + 4\sum_{j=1}^n z_{\beta,j}(k)}, \label{eq:f_direct}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:s_direct}
S(k) = f(\alpha^*(k)) - \frac{1}{2},
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:beta_i_algo}
\beta_i'(k+1) = \max\left\{\beta_i'(k) - \epsilon_2 S(k), -\frac{1}{4} + \delta\right\},
\end{equation}
where $\epsilon_2 > 0$ is the step size, $\delta > 0$ is a small constant ensuring $\beta_i' > -1/4$ for stability. The target bidding coefficient $\alpha_i^*$ follows from the Nash equilibrium condition in Lemma~2 using consensus term $z_\eta$. The consensus estimate $\sum_{j=1}^n z_{\beta,j}(k)$ is obtained via \eqref{eq:beta_sum_consensus}. The error signal $S(k)$ measures the deviation from the Pareto-optimal condition $f = 1/2$. The algorithm is considered converged when the stopping criterion is satisfied:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:stop}
|S(k)| < \tau \quad \text{and} \quad \left\|\sum_{j=1}^n \beta_j'(k+1) - \sum_{j=1}^n \beta_j'(k)\right\| < \tau_\beta,
\end{equation}
where $\tau, \tau_\beta > 0$ are convergence tolerances.

\begin{algorithm}[hbt]
\caption{Distributed Risk-Aware Nash Optimization}
\label{alg:dist_risk_aware}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require Initial $\beta_i'(0)$; step size $\epsilon_2 > 0$; tolerances $\tau, \tau_\beta > 0$
\Ensure Converged values $\{\beta_i'^*, \alpha_i^*\}_{i=1}^n$
\State \textbf{Initialize:} $\alpha_i(0) = 0.5$ for all $i$, $k = 0$
\State \textbf{One-time consensus:} Compute $n$ (using $z_n$), $x_r^o$ (using $z_{xr0}$), and $\eta_i$ (using $z_\eta$)
\While{stopping criterion \eqref{eq:stop} not satisfied}
    \State Compute consensus $z_\beta(k)$ and $\sum_{j=1}^n z_{\beta,j}(k)$ using Equation \ref{eq:beta_sum_consensus}
    \State Compute $\alpha_i^*(k)$ using consensus terms $z_\eta$ and $z_\beta(k)$ (Equation \ref{eq:alpha_direct})
    \State Compute $f(\alpha^*(k))$ using consensus term $z_\beta(k)$ (Equation \ref{eq:f_direct})
    \State Compute $S(k)$ using Equation \ref{eq:s_direct}
    \State Update $\beta_i'(k+1)$ using Equation \ref{eq:beta_i_algo}
    \State $k \leftarrow k + 1$
\EndWhile
\State \Return $\{\alpha_i^*, \beta_i'^*\}_{i=1}^n$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

When risk-averse operators would theoretically require $\alpha_i > 1$, the bidding coefficient is clamped to unity. In such cases, the aggregate risk parameter $\sum\beta_i'$ may not converge exactly to $(1-n)/4$; yet the algorithm still achieves $f(\alpha) = 0.5$ and Pareto-optimal aggregate profit through the saturating equilibrium mechanism. This demonstrates the robustness of the distributed approach: even when heterogeneous risk preferences push some operators to their capacity limits, the collective outcome remains efficient.

These results validate that the proposed distributed algorithm achieves Pareto-enhanced Nash equilibria using only local computations and minimal information exchange. Regardless of initial risk preferences, the algorithm guides all operators toward the collective optimum. In particular, the renewable operators do not require knowledge of other agents' forecasts, cost parameters, or private objectives, yet collectively achieve the same performance as a centralized optimizer. This highlights the practicality of the proposed framework for real-time electricity markets with decentralized renewable participants.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper proposed a risk-aware bidding framework in which the risk sensitivity parameter $\beta_i$ serves as a tunable coordination variable for renewable generation operators in electricity markets. We established that the Pareto-optimal Nash equilibrium is achieved when the aggregate risk parameters satisfy $\sum_{i=1}^n a_2\beta_i = (1-n)/4$, revealing that risk seeking is not merely a behavioral preference but a mechanism to align individual and collective interests without violating incentive compatibility.

A distributed consensus-based algorithm was developed to realize this condition using only local information exchange. Operators do not share forecasts, cost parameters, or private objectives, yet collectively achieve the same performance as a centralized optimizer. Numerical results on a three-operator market confirmed convergence from various initial risk profiles to the Pareto-optimal outcome, yielding approximately 33\% improvement in aggregate profits (from \$16{,}875 to \$22{,}500).

Future work will address network congestion constraints and locational pricing, extend the framework to operators with heterogeneous cost structures and forecast accuracies, and investigate robustness to communication delays and malicious agents in realistic cyber-physical environments.
